An 18-day field course in Thailand for universities and study-abroad providers. Students learn standard wildlife and human-dimensions field methods at eight sites, from cloud forest to a Karen village to the farm edge where elephants and people meet. The curriculum, the sites, and the in-country team are built. Your institution adds the students and the credit.

Human-elephant conflict is the clearest teaching case for a problem with no purely technical solution.

Thailand's wild elephant population has grown to roughly 4,000 over the last 30 years, while about 70% of the species' historical habitat in the country has been converted to other uses. Students watch a well-designed intervention fail for social reasons and a socially strong intervention fail for technical ones, at the sites where both happened.

Four biogeographic zones meet in a country the size of France.

Thailand sits where the Sundaic, Indo-Chinese, Indo-Burmese, and Sino-Himalayan zones converge, and its protected areas are close enough together that 18 days cross a montane cloud forest, a mixed deciduous core protection zone, and a farm-forest interface without a single internal flight.

A growing population inside a shrinking habitat.

Thailand is one of the few parts of the Asian elephant range where the species is expanding. That makes it the right place to teach conservation as practiced: students design and run standard wildlife surveys, sample animal behavior systematically, collect human-dimensions data, and record, manage, and interpret what they gather.

Every methods module carries a social, ecological, and cultural component, and asks who counts as an observer, whose knowledge enters the dataset, and which costs get measured.

What students leave able to do.

  1. Asian elephant ecology, behavior, and individual identification

    Working knowledge of Elephas maximus social structure, communication, foraging ecology, and habitat use. Students identify individuals by photo ID, ear notches, tail characteristics, and tusk and tush morphology, and build ethograms under sustained observation.

  2. Tropical forest ecology and biodiversity monitoring

    Forest structure, species composition, and biodiversity patterns across an elevation gradient, from Doi Inthanon's cloud forest to the evergreen and mixed deciduous forest of the Western Forest Complex and the farm edge, with biogeographic frameworks for species distributions at regional scales.

  3. Conservation field survey methods

    Bird point counts, line transects for mammals, camera trap deployment, and ethogram-based behavioral sampling, with the logic of sampling design, sources of bias, and the interpretation of quantitative data collected under field conditions.

  4. Human-elephant coexistence and conflict mitigation

    Conflict analyzed as a social-ecological problem, not a wildlife management problem alone. Students interrogate the evidence base for early warning systems, beehive fences, crop-switching, physical barriers, and community-based monitoring.

  5. Conservation institutions and governance

    The operational logic, theory of change, and structural constraints of the Department of National Parks, World Heritage management, the state-run Thai Elephant Conservation Center, the community sanctuary KSES, the Friends of the Asian Elephant Hospital, and ZSL's Nature-based Solutions program.

  6. Conservation and communities

    Direct engagement with Karen communities, Thai smallholder farmers, mahouts, park rangers, and ZSL staff. Imposed coexistence, adverse incorporation, and GEDSI as operational tools rather than compliance boxes, with the Billy Rakchongcharoen case and Kaeng Krachan's 2021 UNESCO inscription as the worked example.

  7. Thailand's cultural and political context for conservation

    How elephants function as symbols in Thai statecraft and Buddhist kingship from the Sukhothai period forward, how the 1989 logging ban reshaped the captive population, and how today's institutions inherit those histories. Basic Thai sufficient for respectful community engagement.

  8. Research design, execution, and communication

    Question formulation, study design, data collection in the field, analysis, and written and oral communication of results, carried through a bounded field project and an individual technical brief.

The course runs from faculty-led instruction at the start to student-led critical evaluation, analysis, and synthesis at the end. Prior field research experience is not required; every skill is taught on site. Students need to arrive adaptable, open-minded, and ready to learn.

Eighteen days, eight sites, five phases.

The route runs north to south, from Chiang Mai through the western forest belt to Bangkok, with every transfer overland. Each phase opens with a field question, teaches a method, and treats the social and institutional content as part of the method.

  1. Calibrate Days 1 to 3

    Chiang Mai, Doi Suthep, Doi Inthanon

    Field safety; sampling design; point-count, transect, vegetation, GPS, and data-sheet fundamentals; watershed and cultural-landscape context.

  2. Observe Days 4 to 7

    Kindred Spirit Elephant Sanctuary, Ban Naklang

    Ethograms, focal and scan sampling, individual ID, inter-observer reliability, community-based conservation, mahout livelihoods, Karen Indigenous knowledge.

  3. Compare institutions Days 8 to 9

    Lampang (Thai Elephant Conservation Center), Sukhothai

    Welfare indicators, state and community institutions, working-elephant history, material culture, governance, the politics of evidence.

  4. Apply Days 10 to 15

    Huai Kha Khaeng, Kanchanaburi with ZSL Thailand

    Camera-trap and spatial logic, ranger knowledge, human-dimensions methods, coexistence interventions, participatory monitoring, GEDSI, safeguards.

  5. Synthesize Days 16 to 18, plus post-program

    Bangkok

    QA/QC, analysis, the environmental-justice audit, team presentations, the individual technical brief.

Ground operations, run by people who live here.

Mitria Community Connections runs transport, lodging, and site coordination under the direction of Tyler and Mook, and the program fee covers all of it. Students arrange only their flights.

Ground transport

Two private vans with professional drivers, each refitted to 11 reclining seats, carry the group of 18 and its luggage over the whole route. Both run on factory diesel. Thai law governs passenger vans to 90 km/h, and the travel days are timed to it; the longest legs, Chiang Mai to Ban Naklang and Huai Kha Khaeng to Kanchanaburi, run about four hours.

Lodging and meals

Hotels and guesthouses in the cities, a lodge at Doi Inthanon, a four-night Karen homestay at Ban Naklang, and sanctuary accommodation at Huai Kha Khaeng. Full board from the first group meal to the last; vegetarian and disclosed medical diets accommodated. Camping is optional and the partner's call, with rentals available.

Entry requirements

US citizens enter on a 30-day visa exemption issued on arrival, extendable once by 30 days for personal travel. Each student files the free Thailand Digital Arrival Card within 72 hours before arrival. Passports must be valid for six months beyond entry.

Air travel

Students book their own flights to Bangkok (BKK) and the domestic connection to Chiang Mai (CNX), where the program begins. Airfare sits outside the program fee.

Park and site fees

Included: Doi Inthanon (300 THB per foreign adult), Doi Suthep-Pui (100 THB), the Thai Elephant Conservation Center (200 THB), and ranger fees.

Field support

A licensed Thai coordinator and translator at every community session and interview, a medical-response retainer, first aid, tested communications, and all program and field equipment.

Health, safety, and risk management.

Advisory and route

The route sits inside the U.S. Department of State's Level 1 advisory for Thailand. Elevated guidance concerns the Thai-Cambodian border and the far-southern provinces of Yala, Pattani, and Narathiwat, none of which the itinerary touches.

Rechecked three times

Advisory status, park closures, and insurance coverage are reviewed at the 90-day go/no-go date, 30 days before departure, and immediately before travel.

Who decides what

Your institution retains authority over program approval, conduct, insurance minimums, and cancellation. The program team directs field-method decisions, local logistics, and permit compliance. The driver controls vehicle-safety decisions, and site authorities control access and can stop work.

Health preparation

CDC advises travelers to Thailand to consult a travel health professional at least one month before departure, be current on routine vaccines, discuss hepatitis A and B, typhoid, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, and site-specific malaria risk with a clinician, use mosquito precautions, and follow an animal-bite protocol. The program prescribes no vaccination or medication; those decisions belong to each participant and a clinician.

Medical response

For a life-threatening emergency, Thai EMS is 1669 and stabilization comes first; hospital coordination, institutional notification, and insurer activation run concurrently and never wait for insurer authorization. One responsible adult accompanies the patient while the other maintains supervision of the group. Police 191, fire 199, Tourist Police 1155.

Thai facilities may require advance payment, and the U.S. government should not be assumed to pay or evacuate. Travel insurance with medical evacuation and repatriation is proposed as mandatory before departure.

Staff readiness

Role descriptions, a 24-hour contact tree, and a daily duty schedule protecting driver rest; at least two named field staff with current CPR/AED and wilderness first aid; safeguarding, anti-harassment, consent, and mandatory-reporting briefings aligned to institutional policy; verified guide, operator, driver, and vehicle credentials and insurance certificates; and named backups for Tyler and Mook with instructions for academic continuity and emergency leadership.

Observation first, with no exceptions.

No elephant riding, bathing, or direct-contact entertainment; no performing animals; no captive big-cat handling or selfies. Kindred Spirit's hands-off policy (no touching, feeding, riding, performance, or visitor interference) sets the welfare boundary. The Thai Elephant Conservation Center is a structured comparison with a state institution through approved husbandry and education activities only. In protected areas, work is noninvasive and non-collecting. Any venue that cannot meet this standard is removed rather than described more carefully.
